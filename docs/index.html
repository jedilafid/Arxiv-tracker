Joint Neural Posterior Estimation and Model Selection for Chemical Reaction Networks
Authors: J. Arturo Esquivel, Jorge A. Campos-Gonzalez-Angulo, Sergio Pablo-García, Alán Aspuru-Guzik, Vianey Leos-Barajas
First: 2026-10-13T17:52:39+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-13T17:52:39+00:00
Comments: 40 pages total (11 pages main text, 29 pages appendix), 18 figures. Code available at: https://github.com/Esquivel-Arturo/JNPE
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2 · Code3
Abstract
Simulation-based Bayesian inference for Chemical Reaction Networks (CRNs) must simultaneously solve two computationally demanding problems: discriminating between overlapping reaction pathways and estimating reaction rate constants. Classical approaches, such as Sequential Monte Carlo ABC, rely on handcrafted summary statistics and scale poorly across large parameter spaces. To overcome this, we introduce a neural inference architecture. Our pipeline utilizes shared 1D-CNNs and a permutation-invariant Set Attention mechanism to learn low-dimensional summary statistics from variable-length, multi-experiment time-series data. These statistics simultaneously condition a model selection network to compute posterior model probabilities and model-specific conditional Invertible Neural Networks (cINNs) that estimate parameter posterior distributions. We demonstrate the practical utility of our approach on a real protodeboronation example, illustrating how it handles structural ambiguity and extracts kinetic parameters from competing catalyst deactivation pathways.

Abstract
Joint probabilistic modeling is essential for forecasting irregular multivariate time series (IMTS) to accurately quantify uncertainty. Existing approaches often struggle to balance model expressivity with consistent marginalization, frequently leading to unreliable or contradictory forecasts. To address this, we propose CircuITS, a novel architecture for probabilistic IMTS forecasting based on sum-product networks (SPNs). Our model is flexible in capturing intricate dependencies between time series channels while structurally guaranteeing valid joint distributions. Experiments on five real-world datasets demonstrate that CircuITS achieves superior joint and marginal density estimation compared to state-of-the-art baselines.

Abstract
Financial analysis requires reasoning over heterogeneous signals, including company fundamentals from regulatory filings and trading signals derived from market prices. However, existing financial benchmarks focus almost exclusively on regulatory filings, neglecting market dynamics and cross-signal interactions. We introduce FinTradeBench, a benchmark of 1,400 questions covering NASDAQ-100 stocks over a ten-year window. To holistically evaluate financial reasoning, FinTradeBench includes questions in three reasoning categories: fundamentals-focused, trading-signal-focused, and hybrid questions that require cross-signal reasoning. Our benchmark starts with 150 expert-authored seed questions, which are then scaled to 1400. To ensure reliable evaluation, we create a Core400 subset in which every item is expert-reviewed. In zero-shot (No-RAG) response generation, we observe that all models perform substantially worse on trading-focused questions compared to fundamental-focused questions. To identify failure causes, we evaluate LLMs under controlled settings including retrieval-based baselines, tool-assisted computation, oracle conditions, and time-series-specialized pipelines. LLMs' capabilities to do computation and evidence selection contribute most to reasoning failures. Additionally, retrieval significantly improves trading-signal reasoning but does not improve fundamental reasoning, where identifying relevant signals remains the dominant failure mode. Finally, rather than relying on a small, fine-tuned model for time-series reasoning, it is better to summarize the time-series data and leverage LLMs' reasoning capabilities.

Photonic reservoir computing with complex networks
Authors: Sion Park, Kohei Watabe, Satoshi Sunada, Tomoki Yamagami, Atsushi Uchida

Abstract
Photonic reservoir computing has attracted increasing attention as a fast and low-cost approach for time-series prediction. Photonic reservoir computing utilizes the high speed, broad bandwidth, and spatial parallelism of light. However, the effect of the internal connection structure (network topology) on the computing performance has not been investigated for large-scale photonic reservoirs. In this study, we experimentally and numerically demonstrate photonic reservoir computing using a spatial light modulator to systematically evaluate the relationship between the network topology and the performance of reservoir computing. We introduce complex network structures such as small-world and scale-free network topologies of the internal nodes in the reservoir. We perform the memory capacity measurement and the one-step-ahead prediction task of the chaotic time series to compare the performance. We found that the small-world network exhibits the maximum memory capacity and the best prediction performance. Our numerical calculations reveal that the performance of the time-series prediction can be optimized by changing the rewiring probability of the network and the leak rate of the reservoir. We also implement photonic human brain network as a reservoir, which is designed by the connectomes of human brain activities. We found that the network topology strongly affects the performance of reservoir computing, and the small-world network structure outperforms the other configurations.

Abstract
Photonic reservoir computing is a promising physical machine-learning approach for time-series prediction. Quantization of the reservoir response signal is an important consideration in the implementation of photonic reservoir computing, and the number of quantization bits and sampling frequency need to be optimized to achieve high prediction performance and low energy consumption. However, few studies have investigated the effects of the number of quantization bits and sampling frequency on both prediction performance and energy consumption. In this study, we introduce the concept of approximate reservoir computing with a semiconductor laser by quantizing the reservoir node states and output weights. We numerically evaluate the performance of a chaotic time-series prediction task and estimate the energy consumption per sample using a component-level model. By optimizing the number of quantization bits, the sampling frequency, and the injection current of the semiconductor laser, we achieve a significant reduction in the estimated energy consumption per sample while maintaining prediction performance. We also obtain model-based estimates of the throughput, energy efficiency, and energy per operation of the proposed reservoir-computing system.

Deterministic Prediction as Global Velocity: Flow Matching for Probabilistic Time-Series Forecast

Abstract
Accurate uncertainty quantification is critical for many time series forecasting applications, and recent studies have increasingly adopted generative models to capture predictive uncertainty. Existing approaches often combine deterministic forecasting with generative modeling to improve predictive performance. However, their joint training is typically unstable because the deterministic model is embedded within the generative process, resulting in deep computational graphs and changing the probability transport path. In this work, we propose DV-Flow, a unified flow-matching formulation that incorporates the deterministic predictor as a parallel velocity component rather than as the endpoint of the transport path. This design enables stable end-to-end optimization while subsuming several existing formulations as special cases, allowing direct comparisons among them. Extensive experiments on six real-world datasets demonstrate that DV-Flow consistently improves probabilistic forecasting performance over strong baselines. Our code is available at https://github.com/tufts-ml/dv-flow-time-series.

GALA: Generation-Aware Cross-Modal Alignment for Text-to-Time-Series Synthesis
Authors: Haochen Zhang, Gengwei Zhang, Laura Yao, Nicholas Konz, Tianlong Chen

Abstract
Synthesizing time series from natural language is emerging as the most expressive form of controllable time series generation. However, existing text-conditioned generators either take caption embeddings frozen from off-the-shelf text encoders, or adapt the encoder end-to-end, letting the denoising loss shape the embeddings only as a by-product. In either case, the conditioning representation is never deliberately matched to the signal modality, leaving it ill-suited to guide generation. We address this by introducing GALA: Generation-Aware cross-modaL Alignment for text conditional time series generation. GALA is a two-stage approach that first contrastively couples a pretrained text encoder with a time-series foundation model into a shared embedding space with both encoders adapted to generation by an auxiliary generative loss, and then freezes the resulting caption embedding to drive a flow-matching generator. On TSFragment-600K, spanning four domains and three fragment lengths, GALA sets a new state of the art, ranking first in 30 of 36 metric columns and reaching an average rank of 1.08/1.08/1.42 at lengths 24/48/96 against 1.92/2.00/1.75 for the strongest baseline. We further find that generator-internal text encoders force a trade-off between fidelity and caption adherence, whereas conditioning on the aligned embedding breaks it: FID, CTTP, and JFTSD all improve at once. Ablating the auxiliary loss degrades FID, CTTP and JFTSD together, it indicates the generative term is a necessary component of the alignment rather than an add-on.

Adaptive Patching Is Harder Than It Looks For Time-Series Forecasting
Authors: Federico Zucchi, Yi Xie, Chao Zhang, Thomas Lampert, Ziyue Li
First: 2026-06-02T15:49:06+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-13T15:00:00+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Adaptive patching has been proposed for time-series forecasting: allocate finer patches where the sequence looks locally informative. We compare three adaptive patching methods with tuned uniform counterparts that use one fixed patch size throughout the input, while preserving each method's backbone family and shared training protocol. Across the evaluated long-horizon benchmarks, we observe mostly small accuracy differences and no consistent advantage for adaptive patching; uniform variants generally train faster and avoid adaptive routing at inference. Motivated by these observations, we develop a theoretical framework for budgeted patch allocation through a common resolution-density representation of uniform, nonuniform, and overlapping layouts, independently of the patching rule. Within a separable shared-loss model, we derive an exact criterion for when nonuniform allocation improves on uniform allocation at the same budget. We characterize modeled gains locally through a quadratic surrogate and bound them globally under strong convexity and a bounded loss derivative. The analysis distinguishes where resolution is placed from how much it is redistributed: even an allocation that correctly ranks regions by their weights can redistribute too much resolution and increase modeled loss. Without coupling across time, positive regional weights preserve the minimizers of the shared loss. When the shared loss has a common minimizing rate, the remaining absolute headroom for improvement tends to zero as the uniform rate approaches it. These results characterize the roles of regional weighting, placement, and redistribution strength under the stated model. The empirical findings support evaluating adaptive patching against a tuned uniform baseline and weighing any accuracy gain against the additional training and inference cost of adaptive routing.

Beyond RMSE and Granger Gain: Multi-Horizon Forecast-Error Entropy for Nonlinear Predictive Coupling

First: 2026-10-13T13:40:41+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-13T13:40:41+00:00
Comments: 35 pages, 13 figures, 12 tables. Software and benchmark package: Zenodo, https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23330544

Abstract
We investigate whether the past of an auxiliary time series provides predictive information about a target beyond that contained in the target's own history. We compare matched out-of-sample target-only and target-plus-source forecasts and introduce the forecast-error distribution entropy (FEDE) predictive-causality framework, where predictive causality is understood in the Granger sense: auxiliary history is informative when it improves prediction beyond the target's own past. FEDE quantifies how the full signed forecast-error distribution changes across prediction horizons. Controlled nonlinear benchmarks hide source-target dependence behind nonlinear transformations not supplied to the predictors. LightGBM recovers these hidden channels, whereas Ridge does not. Across four equal-power nonlinearities and four noise levels, the mean response-sensitivity statistic for FEDE remains 29.00, 27.47, 23.22, and 28.67, compared with 14.09, 10.68, 12.67, and 12.71 for mean log-RMSE gain. Directional and source controls separate the true source-to-target channel from the reverse direction and from independent periodic, quasiperiodic, chaotic, and autoregressive auxiliaries. Chronological Granger-style comparisons show strong model-form dependence: a degree-2 polynomial model is exceptionally sensitive to the matched quadratic channel but becomes weak or oppositely aligned for other nonlinearities, while basic convergent cross mapping is weak or directionally inconsistent in this stochastic, partly non-invertible setting. FEDE remains positively aligned across all 108 tested combinations of window length, history length, and maximum forecast horizon. Together, these results establish FEDE as a sensitive and robust horizon-resolved tool that extracts predictive information beyond conventional error-magnitude measures when the nonlinear interaction form is unknown. Code available in Zenodo.

Abstract
Wearable fall detection is hampered by rare fall events, scarce labeled IMU data, and the cost of training deep models on large, imbalanced corpora. Existing methods either rely on hand-crafted features with shallow classifiers, which limits learning from raw signals, or train deep networks on full datasets that require re-sampling to counter class skew and demand substantial data and compute. To address this, we propose a novel Fall detection framework via Normalized-gradient and Embedding matched Synthetic Time-frequency condensation, dubbed FALLNEST, a data-centric approach that distills large, imbalanced IMU datasets into tiny, class-balanced synthetic training sets. Building on dual-domain time-series condensation (CondTSC), FALLNEST introduces a fall-specific 7-channel input with accelerometer vector magnitude, class-balanced K-Means initialization, and normalized gradient matching with gradient clipping, combined with MMD-based embedding alignment and multi-view Fourier-domain augmentation, so that class balance arises from the condensed set itself rather than from re-sampling. We evaluate on SisFall, KFall, and MobiFall using strict subject-wise splits and naturally imbalanced test sets, reporting accuracy, Macro-F1, and PR-AUC over five seeds. A 102K-parameter TinyCNN trained on only 32 to 146 synthetic windows (about only 1% of the training windows) reaches 93.5-95.8% mean accuracy and 0.89-0.98 PR-AUC, with standard deviations of 1.0-1.7% in accuracy, demonstrating the efficacy of our approach.

Abstract
Hierarchical time series forecasting requires generating accurate predictions that are coherent across multiple levels of aggregation. Existing methods either rely on two-stage pipelines with post-hoc reconciliation or on end-to-end models that often lack statistical guarantees. We introduce a unified framework that formulates hierarchical forecasting as a multivariate regression problem in a vector-valued reproducing kernel Hilbert space. By designing structured operator-valued kernels, our approach enforces coherence directly within the hypothesis space, ensuring that all predictions satisfy hierarchical constraints by construction. We further propose tailored kernel constructions for hierarchical data and derive excess risk bounds that characterize generalization performance. Empirical results on synthetic and real-world datasets demonstrate the relevance of the proposed method.

Abstract
Traditional time-series classification methods often operate as black boxes, providing a class prediction without indicating which time point or time interval within the series led to that prediction. To improve temporal interpretability, MILLET (Multiple Instance Learning with Temporal Explanation), which combines an InceptionTime encoder with Conjunctive Pooling, provides both classification and temporal explanations. These results demonstrate that lightweight architectures can maintain strong classification performance and temporal explanations while substantially reducing model size. The proposed variants address specific limitations of the pooling strategy used by MILLET, providing a practical direction for interpretable time-series classification on resource-constrained edge devices.

TStruct: Learning Shared Temporal Structures for Long-Term Time-Series Forecasting
Authors: Yangze Li, Zhengyang Zhou, Qihe Huang, Pengkun Wang, Binwu Wang, Yang Wang
Venue: NeurIPS 2026

Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Long-term time series forecasting requires temporal dependency modeling that remains stable and generalizable under temporal distribution shifts. However, real-world time series are often noisy and non-stationary, making deep learning models prone to fitting incidental temporal interactions in the training data. A key question is whether there exist temporal relationship structures that recur across different temporal positions and variables. We analyze temporal relationship matrices on commonly used time series datasets and find that samples from different temporal positions and different variables exhibit shared dominant temporal relationship structures. This indicates that temporal relationship structures contain patterns that recur stably across samples and variables. To learn such shared structures, we propose \textbf{T}emporal \textbf{Struct}ure Network (\textbf{TStruct}), a simple yet effective network for directly learning shared and generalizable temporal dependencies. Specifically, TStruct directly parameterizes a set of shared temporal dependency matrices and generates normalized combination weights using dynamic sample information and static variable priors. These weights represent a soft allocation of each sample-variable pair over shared temporal dependency structures, enabling the model to generate adaptive temporal dependency matrices under shared structural constraints, rather than fitting incidental interactions in a fully unconstrained manner. Notably, this simple structure-aware design consistently outperforms several strong baselines across all datasets, achieving superior accuracy while maintaining high computational efficiency.

Abstract

Abstract
Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) provides portable and spatially localized measurements of cortical hemodynamics, but differences in channel count, probe layout, signal distribution, and task type hinder joint pretraining across datasets. We introduce GeoNIRS, a geometry-aware framework that uses source-detector locations as a shared spatial reference across heterogeneous probe layouts. Geometry perturbations and pretraining ablations further show that both channel geometry and cross-chromophore reconstruction contribute to the learned representations.

Abstract
We study industrial fault diagnosis as a classification task that assigns sensor time-series windows to predefined health states or fault types. Scarce labels and changing operating conditions make repeated training of specialized classifiers costly. Yet structures such as periodicity, trends, and transients can remain diagnostically informative and be examined through reusable analytical tools. LLM agents can select and interpret these tools, but fixed rules or training-derived guidance may not resolve each signal's ambiguity, while unconstrained interpretation may overstate weak or conflicting results. We introduce Agentic4PDM, a framework that separates adaptive investigation from control over evidence use. LLM agents compare each signal with labeled references from every candidate class, identify unresolved distinctions, and propose follow-up measurements. Experiments on selected bearing and motor diagnosis challenges and an unfiltered industrial test set supplied by our customer show gains in aggregate macro F1 over the evaluated diagnostic and agentic baselines. Ablations further support the value of the governed workflow.

Abstract
Flow matching has emerged as a powerful alternative to diffusion models for generative modeling, but standard conditional flow matching is fundamentally misaligned with partially observed time series: starting from an uninformed Gaussian source forces the model to transport even already-observed entries from noise to the target, wasting capacity on trivially known regions. Abstract

Comments: 46 pages

Abstract
What information does a reinforcement learning policy omit? At each state, a standard policy stores one probability per action. It has no place to record how actions relate to each other, such as which pairs go together and which conflict. We ask when this missing structure matters, and we give exact answers. We store the policy at each state as a density matrix, a positive semidefinite, unit-trace matrix borrowed from quantum information theory. We use it here as a purely classical data structure, computed on ordinary classical hardware. The diagonal holds the usual action probabilities. The off-diagonal entries, called coherences, are new parameters. This gives density-matrix reinforcement learning (DMRL). Coherences change outcomes in two settings. First, suppose a policy is regularized toward a coherent reference policy using matrix relative entropy. The optimum then has coherences that no diagonal reference policy produces, and its action probabilities can depend on the reference policy's coherences. This is relevant to fine-tuning of large language models (LLMs) with a Kullback--Leibler penalty. Second, suppose the agent chooses a unit-vector action and the reward is a quadratic function of that vector. This could arise when an LLM judge scores a weighted consolidation of reasoning paths, or when coupled robotic sensors each respond to a mix of actions. Then some coherent policies earn strictly more expected reward than every classical policy with the same action probabilities, and we give the margin in closed form. In a two-action example, both policies assign probability $0.5$ to each action, yet the classical one earns expected reward $0$ while the coherent one earns $1$, the maximum. Outside these two settings, DMRL matches or transfers the classical results. This is a theory paper. All results are proved, and we report no empirical evaluation.

Abstract

Abstract
Real data are often arbitrary, indirect observations of an unknown underlying process, and those observations can distort its geometry.
